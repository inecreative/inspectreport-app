Home Inspection Checklist: What Inspectors Check, Room by Room

November 8, 2026 · InspectReport AI

A home inspection is a walkthrough with a system: outside in, top to bottom, one area at a time. The inspector is not hunting for perfection — they are checking whether each part of the house does its job, whether anything is unsafe, and what will cost money soon. This checklist follows the same order most inspectors work in, room by room, so you can see what gets looked at and why it ends up in the report.

One thing to know up front: a standard home inspection is visual and non-invasive. The inspector does not open walls, move heavy furniture, or dismantle equipment. They operate what is meant to be operated — faucets, switches, the furnace, the garage door — and they look, test, photograph, and note.

Outside: the exterior and the ground it stands on

Before going inside, the inspector walks the whole perimeter. Water is the theme here — where it lands, where it runs, and whether it gets into the house.

  • Grading and drainage: does the ground slope away from the foundation, or toward it? Standing water, eroded soil, and downspouts that dump at the wall are the findings that come back as damp basements and foundation cracks later.
  • Foundation: visible cracks, bowing, spalling, or signs of past water entry. Hairline shrinkage cracks are common; wide, stepped, or horizontal cracks are the ones that get flagged for a structural specialist.
  • Siding and trim: rot, warping, loose boards, gaps at joints, and paint failure that leaves wood exposed. Brick gets checked for cracked mortar and missing weep holes.
  • Windows and doors: seals, frames, operation, and broken glass. Fogged double-pane glass means a failed seal — the window still works, but its insulation value is gone.
  • Roof, from the ground: missing or curled shingles, sagging lines, damaged flashing around chimneys and vents, and gutters that are full, detached, or draining against the house. If the roof can be walked safely and the inspector's standards allow it, they check it up close; otherwise it is a ground-level and ladder view plus binoculars.
  • Decks, porches, and steps: ledger-board attachment, rot at posts and railings, loose boards, and railings that flex or are missing where a fall is possible.
  • Driveway and walkways: trip hazards from heaved slabs, major cracking, and drainage that directs water toward the garage or the foundation.

Attic: structure, insulation, and ventilation

The attic is where the roof and the house meet, and it shows problems early — leaks, poor ventilation, and insulation gaps all leave marks here.

  • Roof structure: rafters and trusses for sagging, cracked members, or alterations that were never properly supported. Look for water stains on the underside of the sheathing — they mark leaks even when the roof looks fine from outside.
  • Insulation: coverage and depth. Bare patches, compressed insulation, and gaps around recessed lights and ductwork are the usual findings.
  • Ventilation: intake vents at the soffits and exhaust at the ridge or gable. Blocked or missing ventilation traps heat and moisture, which shows up as mold on sheathing or premature shingle wear.
  • Chimney and flue penetrations: flashing from the inside, and clearances where a flue passes through wood framing.
  • Signs of pests: droppings, nesting material, and gnawed wiring or insulation — noted, not investigated; pest identification is a specialist's job.

Basement and crawlspace: the house from below

The lowest level tells the story of water and structure. If the basement or crawlspace is not accessible, that goes in the report as a limitation — the inspector says so plainly rather than guessing.

  • Water entry: stains, efflorescence (white mineral deposits on masonry), rust on the bottom of the water heater or furnace, and any sump pump — is it present, does it run, and where does it discharge?
  • Foundation walls and floor: cracks, bowing, and settlement, read the same way as the exterior foundation check.
  • Floor structure: joists and beams for sagging, notching, or sistered repairs; columns and supports for proper footing and rust.
  • Plumbing and wiring visible below: leaks, improper supports, and exposed or amateur wiring get noted here even though the systems get their own checks upstairs.
  • Moisture and mold: the inspector notes visible moisture and conditions that encourage mold growth. Confirming what kind of mold is present is lab work, not part of a standard inspection.

Garage

  • Door operation: the overhead door opens and closes smoothly, the auto-reverse safety feature works, and the opener's sensors are in place.
  • Fire separation: the wall and door between garage and house, including whether the door is fire-rated and self-closing — this is a safety item, not a cosmetic one.
  • Floor and structure: cracks, settlement, and any signs that vehicles or stored items are hiding damage.
  • Electrical: GFCI protection on outlets, and whether the opener and any sub-panel are wired correctly.

Kitchen

The kitchen packs more systems into one room than anywhere else in the house: water, gas, electricity, and ventilation, all in daily use.

  • Sink and faucet: hot and cold water, drainage speed, leaks under the sink, and the condition of supply and drain lines.
  • Built-in appliances: the dishwasher runs a cycle, the range and oven heat, the microwave works, and the garbage disposal runs. The inspector checks operation, not cooking performance — a dishwasher that runs but does not drain is a finding; a slow oven is usually not, unless it does not heat at all.
  • Ventilation: the range hood vents outside or recirculates, and the fan actually moves air.
  • Outlets: GFCI protection near the sink, and enough outlets that the room is not running on extension cords.
  • Cabinets and counters: secure mounting, water damage under the sink and around the dishwasher, and countertop condition.
  • Flooring and walls: soft spots near the sink and dishwasher, which usually mean a slow leak that has been going on for a while.

Bathrooms

  • Toilet: flushes fully, fills and shuts off, does not rock on its base, and shows no leak at the base or supply line. A rocking toilet usually means a failed wax ring — small fix, real water damage if ignored.
  • Shower and tub: water pressure and temperature, drainage, grout and caulk condition, and any softness in the surrounding wall or floor that points to a leak behind the tile.
  • Sink and vanity: same as the kitchen — supply, drain, leaks, and water damage in the cabinet below.
  • Ventilation: an exhaust fan that vents outside, not into the attic. A fan that dumps moist air into the attic is a classic source of attic mold.
  • Electrical: GFCI protection on every outlet, and a working light.

Bedrooms and living areas

  • Walls, ceilings, and floors: cracks, stains, sagging, and uneven floors. Water stains get traced upward — a stain on a bedroom ceiling usually starts at the roof or the bathroom above it.
  • Windows: every window in the room opens, stays open, locks, and has intact glass and screens. Stuck windows are a safety issue in a bedroom, where the window is a second exit.
  • Doors: latch, close, and do not rub or stick badly enough to signal a shifting frame.
  • Outlets and switches: a representative sample is tested in each room. The inspector does not test every outlet in the house — they test enough to know whether the circuits are behaving.
  • Smoke and carbon monoxide detectors: present, and tested where the inspector's standards call for it. Missing detectors are one of the most common findings in the whole inspection, and one of the cheapest to fix.
  • Stairs and railings: secure handrails, even risers, and no missing balusters where a child could slip through.

Heating and cooling (HVAC)

  • Furnace or boiler: age, general condition, and whether it fires and heats when called. The inspector runs it through a normal cycle; they do not dismantle it or measure efficiency.
  • Air conditioning: the outdoor unit's condition, the indoor coil and drain, and whether cool air actually comes out of the registers. In cold weather the AC may not be tested — running it below its rated temperature can damage it, so that goes in the report as a limitation.
  • Filter and ductwork: a dirty filter is noted, and visible ducts are checked for disconnection, damage, and insulation in unconditioned spaces.
  • Water heater: age, leaks, rust, venting, and whether the temperature-pressure relief valve and its discharge pipe are installed correctly. A water heater past its expected service life is noted even when it still works.

Electrical system

  • Service panel: capacity, labeling, and condition — rust, scorch marks, double-tapped breakers, and aluminum branch wiring are the findings that matter. The inspector does not remove the panel cover if it is unsafe to do so, and says so when they don't.
  • Grounding and bonding: the system is grounded and the main panel is bonded, as far as a visual check can tell.
  • GFCI and AFCI protection: where modern standards require it — kitchens, bathrooms, garages, exteriors — and whether it trips when tested.
  • Visible wiring: exposed, damaged, or amateur work in the attic, basement, and garage.

Plumbing system

  • Water supply: the main shutoff location, visible supply piping material and condition, and water pressure at a fixture.
  • Drainage: fixtures drain at a reasonable rate, and there are no visible leaks at traps and connections.
  • Fuel gas: where present, visible gas lines and connections are checked for leaks by smell and for proper materials and support.
  • Fixtures overall: every fixture in the house gets run — this is the one system where the inspector checks all of them, not a sample.

How the checklist becomes the report

The checklist is the walkthrough; the report is what the buyer and their agent actually use. A good report does three things with what the checklist finds: it shows the problem with a photo, it says where it is and how bad it is, and it says what should happen next — monitor it, get a quote, or bring in a specialist. Findings without a photo are hard to believe; photos without a severity are hard to prioritize.

FAQ

What does a home inspector check first?

Most inspectors start outside — grading, foundation, siding, and roof — then work top to bottom: attic, upper floors, main floor, basement or crawlspace, and the mechanical systems last. The order matters less than covering every area once, in the same sequence, on every job.

Does a home inspection cover the roof?

Yes, visually. The inspector checks the roof covering, flashing, gutters, and penetrations from the ground, a ladder, or by walking it when it is safe and their standards allow it. They do not guarantee the roof's remaining life, and they do not remove shingles to look underneath.

What is the most common finding in a home inspection?

There is no single answer that holds for every house, but the findings that show up again and again are small and fixable: missing or dead smoke detectors, grading that slopes toward the house, failed window seals, GFCI outlets that are missing or do not trip, and slow leaks under sinks. The expensive findings — roof, foundation, and major systems — are rarer, which is exactly why the inspection exists.

How long does a home inspection take?

It depends on the size and age of the house, and on how many findings need photographing and noting. A typical single-family home takes a few hours on site; the report is usually delivered the same day or the next.

What is not included in a standard home inspection?

Pest inspection, mold testing, radon testing, sewer scope, chimney inspection, and specialist evaluations of the roof, foundation, or HVAC system are usually separate services. A standard inspection is a general visual survey; anything it flags as beyond that scope gets referred to the right specialist.

This checklist describes a general residential home inspection as it is commonly performed in the United States. Scope and standards vary by state and by inspector association — InterNACHI and ASHI publish their own standards of practice, and some states license inspectors with their own requirements. Check the standard that applies where you work.
