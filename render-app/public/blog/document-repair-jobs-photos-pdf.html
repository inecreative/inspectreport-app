How Contractors Can Document Jobs with Photos and Send a Clean, Priced PDF

October 31, 2026 · InspectReport AI

If you run a plumbing, roofing, painting, tile, handyman, HVAC, or electrical business, your documentation probably lives in a text-message thread: a few photos, a price typed in a hurry, a customer reply of "ok" — and three weeks later, a disagreement about what was actually agreed. This guide covers a simple habit that prevents most of that: photograph every job before, during, and after, write short notes on site, and send the customer one clean, priced PDF they can approve.

Why text-message threads cause disputes

Text threads feel convenient because they are fast. The problems show up later. Photos get buried under newer messages. The price you quoted sits three screens above the extra work the customer asked for, with nothing connecting them. When a customer says "that was already like that" or "you never told me it would cost more," the thread rarely settles it — you end up scrolling, screenshotting, and arguing from memory.

The core issue is that a thread is a conversation, not a document. There is no single place where the condition you found, the work you propose, and the price all sit together with the customer's approval attached. A short, structured document fixes that: one file, dated, with photos, findings, and prices the customer can review and sign off on before work begins.

Photograph before, during, and after

Before: protect yourself

Before photos record the starting condition — the stained ceiling under the leak, the cracked tiles, the peeling paint, the existing damage near where you will work. They take a minute and they are your evidence if a customer later claims your crew caused damage that was already there. Photograph anything pre-existing within a few feet of the work area, not just the problem itself.

During: show the hidden work

During photos capture what the customer will never see once the wall is closed or the roof is covered: the corroded pipe inside the wall, the rotted decking under the shingles, the wiring you replaced. These photos justify the price better than any explanation, because the customer can see exactly what their money fixed.

After: prove completion

After photos show the finished result from the same angles as the before shots. Paired together, they make the value of the job obvious, they close out the paperwork cleanly, and they give you a record if a warranty question comes up months later.

Write short notes on site, not from memory

Documentation fails when it depends on remembering details at the end of the day. Instead, type a few short notes at the job, in the words you would use out loud: "kitchen sink P-trap cracked, cabinet floor water-damaged," "north slope shingles curled, decking soft near vent," "master bath grout failed, backer board wet." Each note belongs with its photos. Two minutes on site saves twenty minutes of reconstruction later, and the note written next to the problem is always more accurate than the one written from the truck.

Turn photos and notes into a clean, priced PDF

The final step is packaging. A good job document has a consistent shape:

  • The property address, date, and your business name at the top
  • Each finding in plain language, with its photos attached to that finding
  • A severity or priority indicator, so the customer sees what is urgent and what can wait
  • A price estimate next to each item, and a total
  • A place for the customer to approve before work starts

Sent as one PDF, this reads like a professional quote instead of a chat log. Customers can forward it to a spouse, a landlord, or an insurer without anything getting lost, and their approval of that exact document is what protects you.

Change orders and warranty protection

Mid-job surprises are where money gets lost. You open the wall and find damage nobody could see. Instead of a verbal "that'll be extra" over the phone, document the new finding the same way: photo, short note, price, and a quick updated PDF or addendum for the customer to approve before you proceed. When every change is documented the same way as the original job, "you never told me" stops being an argument.

The same records protect you afterward. If a customer questions a repair during the warranty period, your before, during, and after photos plus dated notes show exactly what condition you found, what you did, and what the finished work looked like. That is a much stronger position than a text thread and a memory.

FAQ

What photos should I take on every job?

At minimum: the problem area before you start, any pre-existing damage nearby, the hidden conditions you uncover during the work, and the finished result from the same angles. Attach each photo to the finding it belongs to, and add a short note while you are still on site.

Why send a PDF instead of texting photos and a price?

A PDF is a single, dated document where the findings, photos, and prices sit together and the customer can approve them as one package. A text thread scatters those pieces across a conversation, which is exactly how disputes about scope and price start.

How does job documentation help with change orders?

When you find hidden damage mid-job, document it like the original work — photo, note, price — and get the customer's approval on the updated document before proceeding. Every change order then has the same clear record as the original quote.
