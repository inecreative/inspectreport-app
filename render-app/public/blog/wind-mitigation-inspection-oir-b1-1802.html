Wind Mitigation Inspections: What the OIR-B1-1802 Checks — and Why Photos Decide the Discount

November 24, 2026 · InspectReport AI

A wind mitigation inspection is the one inspection in Florida that can pay for itself. A qualified inspector walks the roof, the attic, and every opening of the house, documents how the home is built to resist hurricane wind, and records it all on the state's official form — the Uniform Mitigation Verification Inspection Form, OIR-B1-1802. The insurer then uses that form to apply credits to the windstorm portion of the homeowner's premium. No other single inspection has such a direct line from the inspector's photos to the homeowner's bill.

The short answer: as of November 2026, the current form is OIR-B1-1802 Rev. 04/26, in effect since April 1, 2026 — every inspection done on or after that date must use it. It records nine wind-resistant features, from the building code the home was permitted under to whether every window and door is protected against windborne debris. The form is valid for up to five years as long as nothing on the house changes. And the detail that decides whether the credits actually land is photographic: every feature claimed needs at least one photo to back it up.

The nine features on the 2026 form

The Rev. 04/26 form added two questions the old form did not have — the home's wind region (based on the design wind speed for the location) and the roof slope — and a section for an IBHS FORTIFIED Home designation. The full list, in the order the form asks:

FeatureWhat the inspector documents
Building codeWhich Florida Building Code edition was in effect when the home (or its re-roof) was permitted
Wind regionThe design wind speed zone where the house sits (new in 2026)
Roof slopeHow steep the roof's primary area is (new in 2026)
Roof coveringThe roofing material, its Florida Product Approval, and when it was installed
Roof deck attachmentHow the plywood or OSB sheathing is nailed to the trusses — nail size and spacing
Roof-to-wall connectionHow the roof is tied to the walls: toe nails, clips, single wraps, or double wraps
Roof geometryHip roof (sloped on all four sides) versus gable or other shapes
Secondary water resistanceWhether a sealed layer — such as a self-adhering membrane or taped deck seams — sits under the covering
Opening protectionWhether every window, door, skylight, and garage door is impact-rated or shutter-protected

Each feature maps to its own credit on the windstorm part of the premium, and the insurer — not the inspector — decides how much each one is worth. The inspector's job is to document the features accurately enough that the insurer can apply them.

The two weakest-link rules that cost homeowners money

Two sections of the form are graded on the worst thing found, not the best — and both are where credits most often disappear.

Roof-to-wall attachment is graded on the weakest connection in the house. The form climbs from toe nails (nails driven at an angle, the weakest) up through clips, single wraps, and double wraps. One toe-nailed truss in an otherwise well-strapped attic can set the answer for the whole home. The inspector has to find and photograph the weakest connector, not the prettiest one.

Opening protection is all-or-nothing. The form rates the house at the level of its weakest opening. If every window has impact glass but one bedroom window or the front door is unprotected, that credit is gone. A single missed opening — a garage door, a skylight, a side door the homeowner forgot about — can erase the largest single credit on the form.

For inspectors, this is the practical lesson: the wind mitigation visit is not a quick roof look. The attic and every opening both matter, and the photos have to prove the weak spots were actually checked, not just the strong ones.

Who can sign the form

Florida law (Section 627.711) limits who can sign an OIR-B1-1802, and an insurer must accept a form signed by any of these:

  • A Florida-licensed home inspector who has completed at least 3 hours of approved hurricane mitigation training, including a proficiency exam
  • A Florida-certified building code inspector
  • A Florida-licensed general, building, or residential contractor
  • A Florida-licensed professional engineer
  • A Florida-licensed architect
  • Any other individual or entity the insurer recognizes as qualified

The person who signs has to inspect the house in person — the only exception is that a licensed engineer or contractor may send a qualified direct employee. And the stakes are real: knowingly submitting a false form to get a discount is a first-degree misdemeanor in Florida.

What it costs, and how long it lasts

Homeowners normally pay for the inspection themselves, and inspectors set their own price — typically $75 to $150 depending on the size of the home, with many companies bundling wind mitigation together with a 4-point inspection when a policy needs both. It can also be free: the state's My Safe Florida Home program provides wind mitigation inspections at no cost to qualifying homeowners (a site-built, single-family home on its own parcel, owner-occupied with a homestead exemption).

The completed form is valid for up to five years from the inspection, as long as no material changes are made to the structure and nobody finds a mistake on it. A new roof, new windows, or added shutters are all reasons to get a fresh one — each can unlock credits the old form never claimed.

The photo mistakes that lose credits

The form requires at least one photo per feature claimed, and this is where reports most often fail on delivery:

  • A feature claimed with no photo behind it. The insurer can reject the credit outright. If the attic was too tight to reach the connector, the honest answer is to say so — not to claim the feature anyway.
  • Photos of the strong spots only. A crisp shot of one double wrap proves nothing if the weakest connection sits unphotographed two bays over. The weakest-link sections need the weakest evidence.
  • Unlabeled, undated, or unlocatable photos. A photo of a nail pattern means little if the insurer cannot tell which part of the roof it came from. Each photo should say what it shows and where.
  • The old form. Any inspection done on or after April 1, 2026 has to be on Rev. 04/26. A report on the 2012 form may still be accepted if it was completed before the change — but a new inspection on the old form will not be.

For inspectors, the fix is a photo-first workflow: shoot every feature at the site, label each photo as it is taken, and build the report from those labeled photos rather than from memory back at the desk. Delivering a wind mitigation report that holds up

The fastest wind mitigation reports follow a simple order: walk the roof and attic first, photograph every connector and nail pattern as you go, then check every opening — including the garage door and skylights — before writing a word. Build the form from the labeled photos, not from what you remember seeing.
