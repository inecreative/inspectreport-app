Commercial Building Inspection Reports: What a CAPEX Summary Adds for Buyers and Owners

November 23, 2026 · InspectReport AI

A residential inspection report answers one question: is this house sound enough to buy? A commercial building inspection report answers a different one: what will this building cost me over the next ten years? The defect list matters, but for a buyer, an owner, or a lender, the list alone is not the decision. The decision is the money — and when it is due.

The short answer: a good commercial report groups every finding by the time horizon in which it will need money — immediate, 1–3 years, 3–5 years, or 5–10 years — and totals the repair cost in each band into a capital-expenditure (CAPEX) summary. Without that table, the client has to add up dozens of line items themselves to learn what the building costs. With it, the report is a capital plan, not just a list of problems.

Why a defect list is not enough for commercial work

On a house, a finding like "roof near end of life — budget $8,000–$12,000" is something the buyer can hold in their head alongside a handful of other items. On a commercial building, the same approach breaks down. A 20,000-square-foot office, warehouse, or retail strip can easily produce 60 or 80 findings: a roof section, three HVAC units of different ages, a parking lot, an elevator, fire suppression, electrical panels, plumbing stacks, and the building envelope. Each finding carries a cost, and each cost falls due in a different year.

Hand a buyer that list with no grouping and one of two things happens. Either they mentally discount the whole thing because they cannot see the total, or they fixate on the scariest single item and miss the pattern — five mid-life systems all coming due in the same two-year window. Neither is a good basis for a purchase decision, a price negotiation, or a maintenance budget.

That is the gap a CAPEX summary closes. It does not add new information; it organizes the information the inspector already collected into the shape a buyer actually thinks in: how much, and when.

The four horizons that make a CAPEX table work

The simplest scheme that works in practice has four bands:

  • Immediate: safety issues and anything that must be fixed before occupancy or before the deal closes — a failed electrical panel, an inoperable fire alarm, a roof leak actively damaging the interior.
  • 1–3 years: systems near the end of their service life that still function — an HVAC unit at year 14 of a 15-year expected life, a parking lot starting to crack.
  • 3–5 years: components with real remaining life but a visible wear pattern — a roof with 4 years left, windows showing seal failure on one elevation.
  • 5–10 years: long-horizon items the buyer should budget for but not panic about — a boiler with half its life left, a facade that will need repointing within the decade.

Two rules keep the table honest. First, every finding that carries a cost gets exactly one horizon — no finding sits in two bands, and no costed finding sits in none. Second, the horizon describes when the money is spent, not how serious the defect is. A serious defect that can wait three years belongs in the 3–5 band; a minor defect that needs fixing now belongs in immediate. Mixing severity and timing is the most common way a CAPEX table goes wrong.

What the summary table should show

A CAPEX summary is a small table, and it works best when it stays small. One row per horizon, with the number of findings and the total estimated cost in that band, plus a grand total:

Time horizonFindingsEstimated cost
Immediate3$18,500
1–3 years7$42,000
3–5 years5$26,000
5–10 years4$31,000
Total19$117,500

The numbers in that table are illustrative — a made-up example to show the shape, not a claim about any real building. What matters is the shape itself: the buyer sees at a glance that $18,500 is due now, $42,000 is due within three years, and the ten-year total is $117,500. That is the number they take to their lender, their partner, or their seller negotiation.

The table should also be automatic. If the inspector has to re-add the numbers by hand after writing the findings, two things go wrong: the table drifts out of sync with the findings whenever a cost changes, and the inspector stops updating it. A report tool that builds the table from the findings — adding each costed finding into its horizon band the moment the finding is written — keeps the summary true without extra work.

How this changes the inspection itself

Working with horizons changes what you do on site, in a good way. When you know every finding needs a time band and a cost, you stop writing vague findings like "monitor the HVAC" and start writing findings with a decision attached: "HVAC unit 2, installed 2011, expected life 15 years — budget $9,500 replacement in 1–3 years."

It also changes the conversation with the client. A buyer who sees "roof: $28,000 in 3–5 years" asks a different question than one who sees "roof: near end of life." The first asks whether the seller will credit it or whether the loan covers it. The second asks whether the roof is about to fail. The horizon turns a worry into a budget line — which is exactly what a commercial buyer needs.

For inspectors moving from residential to commercial work, this is also the clearest sign that the job is different. Residential reports are organized by system and severity; commercial reports are organized by system, severity, and time. If your report tool only does the first two, you will be building the third by hand in a spreadsheet after every job.

What to check before taking on commercial jobs

If you are considering commercial inspections, check three things in your report workflow before you accept the first one:

  • Can findings carry a time horizon? Your home inspection report software should let you tag each finding with when the repair is due — immediate, 1–3, 3–5, or 5–10 years — not just how severe it is.
  • Does the CAPEX table build itself? The summary should total itself from the findings and their costs. A hand-maintained table will be wrong by the second revision.
  • Do the costs travel with the findings? The estimate attached to each finding should appear in the report, in the PDF the client receives, and in the summary table — the same number in all three places, with no retyping.

The bottom line

A commercial inspection report without a CAPEX summary is a list of problems with no price tag on the future. Add the four horizons, total the costs in each band, and the same inspection becomes a capital plan the buyer can actually use — to negotiate, to borrow against, and to budget from. The inspection work is the same either way; the difference is whether the report hands the client the total, or makes them do the math themselves.
