4-Point Inspection Reports: What Insurers Ask For and How to Deliver Them Fast

November 23, 2026 · InspectReport AI

A 4-point inspection is the shortest report an inspector writes — and one of the most consequential. It covers only four systems in a house: the roof, the electrical system, the plumbing, and the heating and air conditioning. One to two pages, a set of photos, and a signature. Yet for an older home, that short report can decide whether the owner gets insurance at all.

If you write home inspection reports, the 4-point is a service worth offering well. It is fast to perform, it is usually requested on a deadline — a buyer closing in days, an owner renewing a policy — and the audience is not the homeowner. It is an underwriter who has never seen the house and wants one answer: is this home likely to produce a claim?

What a 4-point inspection actually is

A 4-point inspection is a condition report on four systems: roof, electrical, plumbing, and HVAC. The inspector records the age, type, and condition of each system and photographs them. The report is written for underwriting, not for the buyer. The sample form from Citizens Property Insurance Corporation, Florida's state-created insurer, states the information "is used to determine insurability and is not a warranty or assurance of the suitability, fitness or longevity of any of the systems inspected."

It is not a full home inspection. A full inspection walks the whole house — structure, exterior, interior, appliances, grading, and more — and produces a long report for the buyer. A 4-point looks at four systems only and produces a short form for the insurer. Homeowners sometimes confuse the two, so it is worth saying plainly in your scheduling call: the 4-point will not tell them whether the house is a good buy. It will tell their insurer whether the house is an acceptable risk.

When insurers ask for one

There is no single law that sets a 4-point age threshold. Each insurer writes its own underwriting rule, and the rules converge around the age of the home. The clearest published example is Citizens Property Insurance, the largest residential carrier in Florida, where 4-point inspections are most common. As of November 2026, Citizens requires a 4-point inspection on new applications for homes more than 20 years old, and its underwriters may ask for one at renewal case by case.

Most private carriers use a later trigger — commonly 25 to 30 years — and ask for an updated report every few years at renewal. Beyond 40 years, the requirement is effectively universal: almost no carrier will write a home built before the mid-1980s without a fresh 4-point in hand.

How fresh the report must be is up to each insurer. Florida's Insurance Consumer Advocate notes that reports are generally dated within 30 days before the insurance application, so a 4-point done six months ago may not satisfy a carrier even if nothing in the house has changed.

What the inspector documents in each system

Roof

The roof is the section that most often decides the outcome. The inspector records the covering material, the approximate age, the condition, evidence of past or active leaks, and the estimated remaining useful life. Carriers have become specific about age: since Florida's 2022 property insurance reforms, an insurer cannot refuse to write or renew a policy solely because of roof age if the roof is less than 15 years old. Once a roof is 15 years or older, the owner has the right to an inspection showing at least five years of remaining useful life, and a carrier that receives that inspection cannot refuse on age alone. Citizens applies its own thresholds on top of that: shingle and other soft roofs older than 25 years, and tile, metal, slate, and concrete roofs older than 50 years, require a roof inspection form documenting five years of remaining life before a policy is written.

Electrical

The electrical section records the panel type and age, the service amperage, the wiring type, and any visible hazards. The items underwriters flag most are cloth wiring, active knob-and-tube wiring, single-strand aluminum branch wiring, and visible hazards such as scorching or double taps. Citizens requires at least 100-amp service on new applications. A photo of the open panel is part of the minimum photo set on the Citizens form — underwriters want to see the panel, not just read about it.

Plumbing

The plumbing section records the supply and drain pipe materials, the age and condition of the water heater, and any active leaks. Polybutylene piping is the big one: Citizens does not insure new-business homes over 20 years old with polybutylene piping. The water heater matters too — the Citizens form asks for a photo of the water heater including its temperature-pressure relief valve.

HVAC

The HVAC section records the age, type, and condition of the heating and cooling equipment, and whether it is working as intended. The underwriter's question is practical: will the air handler drain into the ceiling, will the system fail in the first year of the policy, and is the equipment past its useful life?

What most often holds up coverage

The same short list shows up again and again: a roof at the end of its life with no remaining-life documentation, an old or hazardous electrical panel, aluminum branch wiring, polybutylene pipe, an active leak, and a water heater past its service life. None of these are surprises to an experienced inspector — which is why the value of the 4-point is not just the form, it is the inspector telling the homeowner, on the spot, which of these items will cost them the policy and what fixing it would take.

When a system fails, say so in plain language and say what would change the answer. "Roof has less than five years of remaining life" is a verdict. "Roof has less than five years of remaining life; a roofer's remaining-life inspection or a replacement would satisfy the carrier" is a verdict with a path forward, and it is the version the homeowner's agent can actually use.

Who can sign a 4-point, and what photos it needs

The Citizens sample form must be completed, signed, and dated by a verifiable Florida-licensed professional — a general, residential, or building contractor, a building code inspector, or a home inspector are the examples it gives. Florida-licensed architects, professional engineers, and building code officials are also accepted if their license can be verified. A trade-specific professional, such as an electrician, may sign only the section for their trade.

The minimum photo set on the Citizens form includes each side of the home, each slope of the roof, the open electrical panel, and the water heater. In practice, photograph more than the minimum. A photo of every data plate, every label, and every defect you describe is what keeps the underwriter from coming back with questions — and questions are what kill the deadline.

4-point vs. wind mitigation: different forms, different jobs

Many of the same older Florida homes need both a 4-point and a wind mitigation inspection, and homeowners mix them up. The distinction is simple: a 4-point inspection decides whether the homeowner can get insurance; a wind mitigation inspection decides how much discount they get on the premium. Different forms, different purposes. Doing both at one visit is standard practice and usually costs the homeowner less than two separate trips — offer the bundle when you schedule the 4-point.

Delivering a 4-point fast: the workflow

The 4-point is a speed job. The homeowner or agent usually needs it within days, and the underwriter needs it complete the first time — a form that comes back with questions is a form that missed its closing. The workflow that works:

  1. Photograph everything at the point of inspection. Data plates, panel interiors, the water heater label, each roof slope, each side of the house. Photos taken later, from memory, are how details get missed.
  2. Record age, type, and condition per system — nothing more. The 4-point is a form, not an essay. Short, structured findings with a severity call beat paragraphs.
  3. Put a repair cost next to any deficiency. The underwriter decides insurability, but the homeowner decides what to fix, and a cost turns a defect into a decision.
  4. Send a clean PDF the same day. The report is one to two pages; there is no reason it should take a week. Same-day delivery is the service that gets you the next referral from the agent who sent this one.

  • Four systems only: roof, electrical, plumbing, HVAC — age, type, condition, photos.
  • Expect the request on homes over 20 years old (Citizens) and 25–30 years old (most private carriers); effectively universal past 40 years.
