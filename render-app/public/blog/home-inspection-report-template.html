Home Inspection Report Template: What a Complete Report Includes

October 31, 2026 · InspectReport AI

A good home inspection report answers three questions before the reader finishes the first page: what was inspected, what is wrong, and what should happen next. Everything else exists to support those three answers with evidence. If you are building your own report template — or judging whether your software's template is complete — use this checklist. A report missing any of these sections will feel thin to clients and agents, even if the inspection itself was thorough.

The short answer: a complete report has a cover and property identification, a scope and limitations statement, an executive summary, a section for every major system and area, findings in a consistent format with photos, and a closing section with signature and disclaimers.

1. Cover and property identification

Every report starts with the facts that identify the job: the property address, the inspection date, the client's name, and the inspector's name and company. Include the inspection type, weather and occupancy conditions if they affected the inspection, and the names of everyone who receives the report. Agents and attorneys will search for this page first when a question comes up months later, so make it complete and unambiguous. A cover photo of the house itself immediately tells the reader this document is about this property, not a generic form.

2. Scope and limitations, up front

Before any findings, state what the inspection covered and what it did not. A home inspection is a visual, non-invasive examination of accessible systems — it is not a code-compliance inspection, not a warranty, and not an appraisal. Say so plainly, in the report itself, not only in a separate agreement the client may never reopen.

This section should name the standard of practice you followed and list any areas that were not accessible (a locked attic hatch, a crawlspace blocked by stored items, a roof too steep to walk) and any systems not operated, with the reason.

3. The executive summary

The summary is the most-read part of the report, so write it as if it were the only part many readers will read — because for some, it is. It should list every significant finding in one place, ordered by importance, with a one-line description, the location, and the recommended action for each. It should not introduce findings that do not appear in the body of the report, and it should not bury urgent items below minor ones.

A useful discipline: if a finding is not important enough for the summary, ask whether it belongs in the body at all. A summary padded with trivial items teaches the reader to skim — and skimming is how a safety issue gets missed. Keep it short, ordered by severity, and honest about what needs attention now versus what can wait.

4. Systems and areas, room by room

The body of the report walks the property in a consistent order, so a reader can find any item without hunting. A standard order covers:

  • Exterior — roof covering, gutters and drainage, siding, grading, driveways, decks and porches.
  • Structure — foundation, visible framing, floors, walls, and ceilings.
  • Roof and attic — covering, flashing, ventilation, and insulation.
  • Plumbing — supply and drain piping, water heater, fixtures, and visible leaks.
  • Electrical — service panel, wiring type, outlets, and GFCI/AFCI protection.
  • Heating and cooling — furnace or heat pump, air conditioning, distribution, and filters.
  • Interior — rooms, doors, windows, stairs, and installed appliances.
  • Garage and crawlspace/basement — where many of the most consequential defects live.

The exact order matters less than consistency: use the same order on every report, and clients and agents learn where to look. For each area, record what was inspected and the condition found — including areas with no defects, since a section with nothing but silence reads like a section that was skipped.

5. Findings, written in a consistent format

Each finding is the unit of the report, and a template earns its keep here. Every finding should carry the same five elements:

  1. Title — plain words naming the defect: "Active leak at kitchen sink drain."
  2. Location — the room and position, specific enough that a contractor can walk straight to it.
  3. Description — what you observed, in client-friendly language, without speculation about causes you could not verify.
  4. Photos — a wide shot for context and a close-up of the defect, captioned.
  5. Recommendation — what should happen next, and by whom: repair by a licensed plumber, evaluation by a structural engineer, monitor at the next service visit.

Two more elements make findings genuinely useful: a severity rating and a repair-cost estimate. Severity tells the reader how urgently to act — urgent or safety, repair, or monitor — and it must mean the same thing on every report, or it means nothing. Cost estimates should be labeled plainly as planning ballparks, not contractor quotes, but clients consistently say they are the most useful part of a finding: a defect with a dollar range attached is one they can plan around. 6. Photos that prove, not decorate

Photos are evidence, not illustration. A finding with no photo asks the reader to take your word for it; a finding with a blurry close-up and no context asks them to guess where it is. The reliable pattern is wide shot first, close-up second, and a caption stating what the photo shows. Photograph cleared items too — a clean panel and a working GFCI end arguments before they start — and keep photos in the finding they belong to, not in an appendix nobody opens.

7. Closing: signature, disclaimers, and next steps

Close the report with the inspector's signature and license or certification information where applicable, a restatement of the key disclaimers (visual inspection, accessible areas only, estimates are not quotes), and a short "what to do next" recap: urgent items first, then repairs, then monitoring items. Note any recommended follow-up inspections — a sewer scope, a radon test, a specialist evaluation — so they are not lost inside the body of the report.

A complete report also has a delivery story: sent the same day, as a PDF the client can open on a phone and forward to their agent and contractors. A perfect report delivered four days late has already cost you the referral.

Common template mistakes

  • No summary, or a summary that lists everything equally. Readers cannot tell what matters. Order by severity.
  • Missing "no defects observed" notes. Empty sections look skipped, not clean.
  • Findings without locations or photos. A contractor cannot price what they cannot find.
  • Jargon without translation. "Double-tapped breaker" means nothing to most buyers; add the plain-language sentence.
  • Costs or severity that change from report to report. Inconsistency erodes trust faster than any single error.
  • Scope buried at the back. Limitations belong up front, where they frame everything that follows.

A template you can start from

If you are building your own template, start with the seven sections above and resist adding more until you have written ten reports with them. Most inspectors discover their template needs fewer sections and better findings, not more pages. The template's job is to make the structure automatic, so your attention stays on judgment: what you saw, how serious it is, and what the client should do about it.

FAQ

How long should a complete home inspection report be?

Length follows the house, not a target. A small condo with few defects may run a dozen pages; a large older home can run several times that. What matters is completeness per section, not page count.

Should the summary include every finding?

No — it should include every significant finding. Minor maintenance notes belong in the body. A summary that lists everything equally teaches readers to skim, which is how urgent items get missed.

Do repair-cost estimates belong in a report template?

Yes, labeled clearly as planning estimates rather than contractor quotes. Clients use them to prioritize and negotiate, and agents expect them. Keep your ranges consistent across reports.

Can one template work for every property type?

One structure can, with property-specific sections added or removed as needed — a condo has no roof section of its own, a rural property may need a well and septic section. Adjust the systems list, not the format, for each property.
