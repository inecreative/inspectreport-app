Home Inspection Report Software for Multi-Inspector Firms: Flat Pricing vs Per-Inspector Fees

November 23, 2026 · InspectReport AI

Solo inspectors shop for home inspection report software on the headline price. Multi-inspector firms can't — because the headline price is almost never the price a team pays. The moment you hire your second inspector, some tools add a seat fee that repeats every month, forever. Others charge by headcount tier. A few charge one flat rate no matter how many inspectors you have. The difference between those models is the difference between a bill that grows with your team and one that doesn't.

The short answer: as of November 2026, Spectora costs $109 a month for the first inspector and $99 a month for each additional one — so a 2-person firm pays $208, a 3-person firm $307, and a 10-person firm $1,000 a month. InspectForge prices by headcount tiers instead: $39 a month solo, $69 for up to 3 inspectors, $99 for up to 10, on standard pricing. HomeGauge is $89 a month for a solo inspector and does not publish multi-inspector pricing. InspectReport AI is $9 a month flat per account with no per-inspector fees, whatever the headcount.

What per-inspector pricing does to hiring

The seat fee is not just a line item; it changes how a firm thinks about growth. Every hire carries a software cost before the new inspector's first job — $99 a month on Spectora, every month, whether the hire is fully booked or still training. That makes a firm slower to hire a second inspector, slower to bring on a part-timer for the busy season, and slower to keep a semi-retired inspector on for a few jobs a month. The software bill becomes a small tax on every hiring decision.

Tiered pricing softens that. On InspectForge, the second and third inspectors cost nothing extra — the firm moves from Starter to Pro once, at $69 a month standard, and the third inspector rides free. The tax returns at the tier boundary: the fourth inspector pushes the firm to Business at $99 a month. The bill still grows with the team, but in steps, not per head.

Flat pricing removes the tax entirely. At $9 a month per account with no per-inspector fees, the tenth inspector costs the firm the same as the first. For a firm owner, that is the whole appeal: the software line never has to be part of the hiring conversation.

What to check beyond the price when you add inspectors

Price is the easy comparison. The harder one is whether the tool keeps the firm's reports consistent as more people write them. Four things to check before the second inspector's first solo job:

  • Shared templates. Every inspector should start from the same report structure, so a client gets the same report no matter who walked the house. If each inspector builds their own template from scratch, the firm's reports drift apart within a month.
  • Consistent severity and pricing language. One inspector's "monitor" is another's "repair now." A shared set of severity badges and a shared price book for repair-cost estimates keep findings comparable across the team — and keep agents from learning which inspector writes the softer report.
  • Review before delivery. In a solo practice, the person who writes the report sends it. In a firm, someone should read a new inspector's report before the client sees it. Whatever tool you use, the review step has to exist as a habit, not a hope.
  • One brand on every report. The report a client receives should carry the firm's name and contact details, not the individual inspector's. That is what makes the firm — rather than the inspector — the thing clients remember and recommend.

None of those four is a pricing feature. They are workflow habits, and they matter more than the seat fee once the team passes three people. A cheap tool with inconsistent reports costs more in lost referrals than a pricey tool with a disciplined review step.

The honest counterpoint: the seat fee buys a platform

The comparison above is a report-writing comparison, and Spectora's per-inspector price is not only a report-writing price. The $109 base plan includes scheduling, online booking, agreements, payments, and QuickBooks integration; the Advanced add-on adds the CRM, team management, and PaySplits that a growing firm actually uses. A 5-person firm paying $505 a month is buying an office system, not just a report writer — and if the firm uses the scheduling and payments every day, the seat fee is paying for itself in admin time.

InspectForge's tiers are the same kind of deal at a lower price: the Business tier's $99 a month includes scheduling, a CRM, payment collection, agreements, and a public website with booking, on top of the report writer. For a firm that wants the platform, the tier price is fair.

InspectReport AI does one job: turn site photos and short notes into a finished report with severity badges and repair-cost estimates, plus priced estimates for repair pros — and export a clean PDF. It does not do scheduling, CRM, or payments. A firm that needs the platform should pay the platform price. A firm that only needs the report should not pay a per-inspector fee for a platform it never opens.

The bottom line

When you are solo, the cheapest headline wins. When you hire, the pricing model wins — or loses. Per-inspector pricing makes every hire more expensive than the salary. Tiered pricing makes the fourth and eleventh hires expensive. Flat pricing makes none of them expensive. Before you hire your second inspector, run the team-size math on your current tool: multiply the seat fee by the team you plan to have in two years, not the team you have today. If that number surprises you, the pricing model — not the vendor — is the thing to reconsider.
