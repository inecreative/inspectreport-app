For Roofers: How to Write a Repair Quote Customers Approve Faster

November 11, 2026 · InspectReport AI

A roofing quote usually fails for a reason that has nothing to do with the price. The customer gets a one-line number — "roof repair: $2,400" — and has no idea what it covers, what it leaves out, or why it costs that much. So they do the rational thing: they wait, they ask a second roofer, and the job goes quiet. The quotes that get approved fast are not always the cheapest. They are the ones the customer can understand in five minutes.

A quote is a sales document as much as a price. It has to answer three questions before the customer will say yes: what is wrong, what exactly are you going to do about it, and what happens if I approve today. Get those three answers on the page, with photos, and the approval usually follows.

Start with the problem, in photos

Most customers have never been on their own roof. They cannot picture "flashing failure at the valley" or "three missing shingles on the south slope." A photo of the actual problem does the explaining for you — and it does something a paragraph cannot: it proves you were up there and saw it yourself.

Take a photo of every problem you are quoting, and put the photo next to the line it belongs to. A leak stain on the ceiling is worth including too, because it connects the roof problem to the damage the customer already lives with. When the customer can see the cracked boot, the lifted shingle, and the stain it caused, the price stops looking arbitrary.

Break the price into lines the customer can read

A single total invites a single reaction: is that too much? Line items invite a different reaction: that looks fair, and I can see where the money goes. Split the quote into the work, not the invoice codes:

  • Each repair as its own line. Replace 12 damaged shingles, re-flash the chimney, reseal the vent boot — each with its own price. The customer can approve the whole job or ask about one line, instead of rejecting the whole number.
  • Materials named, not lumped. "Architectural shingles, matching colour" beats "materials $600." Naming the material tells the customer you are not cutting corners, and it stops the "what brand is that?" phone call.
  • Optional work kept optional. Gutter cleaning, a ridge vent upgrade, or a full-slope re-shingle can sit as optional add-ons the customer ticks, rather than inflating the main price. Optional lines let the customer say yes to the repair now and yes to the extra later.
  • A stated scope and exclusions. What is included, and what is not — decking replacement if rot is found, for example, priced as a per-sheet rate rather than a surprise. Customers approve faster when the unknowns are named up front.

Make approval a one-tap decision

The gap between "looks good" and "approved" is where most quotes die. If approval means printing, signing, scanning, and emailing back, the customer will do it tomorrow — and tomorrow becomes next week. Send the quote as a link the customer opens on their phone, ticks the optional items they want, and approves with their name. No account, no download, no printer.

Speed matters here because roofing problems get worse. A leak that costs $800 to fix this week can cost far more once the decking rots, and the customer knows it. A quote that is easy to approve today is a quote that gets approved before the next rainstorm does the deciding for them.

What to leave out of the quote

  • Pressure. "Price valid 7 days" is honest and useful; "price goes up tomorrow" is not, and customers can tell the difference.
  • Jargon without a photo. Technical terms are fine when the photo beside them shows what the term means. Without the photo, they read as a way to hide the price.
  • A second visit to explain it. If the quote needs a phone call to make sense, the quote is not finished. The document should carry the whole argument.
